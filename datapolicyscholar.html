Visualizing the Influence of Income on Government Spending Attitudes

I explored how individual income and county-level income metrics influence attitudes towards government spending on social services in the United States. This interactive data visualisation project is a continuation of my capstone research note for the Data and Policy Summer Scholar Program at University of Chicago's Harris School of Public Policy in July 2024.

About

Income is a fundamental socioeconomic factor that shapes individuals’ life experiences and opportunities. A study examining how exogenous changes in income affect political attitudes, particularly views on redistribution and government spending1, found that self-interest influences policy preferences most strongly when the personal financial implications are clear.

Due to rising income inequality2, the systematic influence of income on people’s views on social programs has major implications for politics and policymaking, as there could be a sharp divergence of policy preferences between the rich and the poor. This could exacerbate political polarization and make it harder to reach compromises on government spending.

Therefore, for my capstone project in UChicago's Data and Policy Summer Scholar program, I wanted to understand the relationship between income characteristics and attitudes towards government spending on social services.

I merged data from the American National Election Studies survey and county-level economic indicators from Chetty et al. (2014), and used a fixed effects model to control for unobserved heterogeneity across states and years*. I processed the results using R's dplyr and sf packages to generate GeoJSON files, which I then used to create interactive visualizations with D3.js.

* The data spans from years 1990-2000, and states with fewer than 30 responses are shown in gray due to insufficient data for reliable statistical analysis.

Findings

There is a clear negative relationship between one’s income level and support for government spending on social services. Higher income individuals are less supportive of social spending, likely because they expect to benefit less directly from such programs. This self-interested attitude is apparent across the income distribution and social class hierarchy.

However, our model has limited explanatory power as other political, social, and demographic variables not captured here also shape views on the social safety net. Further research could examine a wider range of contextual factors and individual characteristics to develop a more complete model of the complex dynamics influencing these attitudes.
